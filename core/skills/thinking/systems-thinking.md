# Systems Thinking

A system is more than the sum of its parts. First-principles decomposes; this traces interactions — complementary, not competing.

## When to apply

Multiple components with feedback between them; cause and effect separated by time or space; an intervention produced a surprising downstream effect; a symptom keeps recurring despite repeated fixes (likely structural, not an event).

## The Iceberg (Meadows) — most leverage sits at the bottom, most attention sits at the top

1. **Events** — what happened (a pipeline failed today)
2. **Patterns** — what's been happening over time (pipelines fail every winter)
3. **Structure** — what produces the pattern (insulation specs assume mild winters; procurement rewards lowest unit cost)
4. **Mental models** — what beliefs shape the structure ("we've always procured this way")

Fixing at level 1 is firefighting. Fixing at level 3 changes the pattern. Changing level 4 reshapes the structure.

## Leverage points (Meadows, descending power)

Paradigms → goals → power to change structure → self-organisation → rules → information flows → feedback-loop gain → material stocks and flows → numbers (parameters, taxes, standards — most-targeted, least powerful). Most effort goes into the bottom; most actual change comes from the top.

## Four lenses to force on any system

**Stakeholders** (who has stakes, what motivates them) · **interconnections** (how components and stakeholders connect) · **boundaries** (where the system boundary is drawn, and what that excludes) · **influences** (what drives it in particular directions).

Use a causal-loop diagram when the question is which loops are reinforcing vs. balancing; stocks-and-flows when you need to model dynamic behavior, not just relationships — otherwise a quick systems map (components + explicit boundary) is usually enough.
